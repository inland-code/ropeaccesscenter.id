This question reaches our desk almost weekly, from two directions at once: technicians starting a career, and HSE managers who must satisfy a tender requirement. The answer differs for each.

## What each scheme actually buys you

**IRATA International** sells cross-border recognition. An IRATA card reads the same way in Jakarta, Aberdeen, and Perth. Multinational oil and gas operators nearly always require it, and its three-level structure gives a clear career path with firm logged-hours requirements.

**BNSP** sells national legitimacy. On government projects, state-owned enterprise work, and for many local contractors, the BNSP competency certificate is the document an auditor checks. The TKPK scheme maps directly onto Ministry of Manpower Regulation 9/2016, which gives it real standing in front of a labour inspector.

**SPRAT** is IRATA’s North American counterpart. Demand in Indonesia is limited — it usually appears when the ultimate client is an American company, or when a technician plans to work there.

## For technicians: where to start

If you are starting out and want the widest possible market, start with IRATA Level 1. The reasoning is simple: the best-paying rope access work in Indonesia is offshore oil and gas, and they ask for IRATA.

But do not stop there. Add BNSP TKPK once you begin taking onshore work. The cost is small relative to the value of the projects it opens up.

## For HSE managers: read the contract, not the brochure

The most expensive mistake we see is certifying an entire team against a scheme the client never asked for. Before booking training, check three things: the competency clause in the contract, the operator’s site induction requirements, and the standards referenced in the tender documents.

If all three say IRATA, BNSP will not substitute — and the reverse holds too.

## What does not change, whichever scheme

Any certificate only states that someone met a standard on the day they were assessed. What keeps people alive is daily habit: pre-use equipment checks, a rescue plan that has actually been tested, and a culture in which anyone may stop the work.
