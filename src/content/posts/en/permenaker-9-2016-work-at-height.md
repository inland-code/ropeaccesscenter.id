Any job where someone can fall — a building facade, a telecom tower, a tank, a factory roof — falls under one main rule: **Ministry of Manpower Regulation No. 9 of 2016** on Occupational Safety and Health for Work at Height. This article summarises it in practical terms. For legal purposes, always refer to the official text listed in the references.

## The five mandatory elements

Regulation 9/2016 requires work at height to meet five elements:

1. **Planning** — risks are assessed before work starts, including avoiding work at height where possible and planning for emergencies.
2. **Work procedures** — written steps that every team member understands, from rigging to de-rigging.
3. **Safe work techniques** — methods that prevent falls, such as the [two-rope system used in rope access](/en/what-is-rope-access/).
4. **PPE, fall protection devices, and anchors** — equipment that meets standards, is inspected, and is connected to strong [anchor points](/en/rope-access-anchor-points/).
5. **Workers** — the people doing the job must be competent and hold proof of competency.

The five depend on each other. The best equipment is useless without a procedure, and the best procedure is useless if the workers are untrained.

## Competency: TKBT and TKPK

The regulation sets qualifications and competency requirements for two groups:

- **High-structure workers (TKBT)** — who climb structures such as towers, steel frames, and scaffolding. See [TKBT Level 1 vs Level 2](/en/tkbt-level-1-vs-level-2/).
- **Rope access workers (TKPK)** — who work suspended on ropes, at [TKPK Level 1](/en/tkpk-level-1-training/), [Level 2](/en/tkpk-level-2-training/), and [Level 3](/en/tkpk-level-3-training/).

Competency is proven through training, a competency test, and a certificate plus K3 licence issued by the Ministry of Manpower. Not sure which path fits? Read [TKPK or TKBT](/en/choosing-rope-access-certification/).

## What it means for employers

For building owners, contractors, and industrial firms, compliance comes down to three practical things: keeping planning and procedure documents, making sure equipment has an [inspection](/en/rope-access-ppe-inspection/) history, and only assigning workers whose certificates and licences are valid. These are also what auditors ask for first — see our [audit checklist](/en/work-at-height-audit-checklist/).

One element is often forgotten: the emergency plan. A worker hanging unconscious on a rope must be brought down in minutes, not hours. That is why we stress a [tested rescue plan](/en/tested-rescue-plan/) and offer [standby rescue services](/en/standby-rescue-services/).

## How it relates to Decree 45/2008

Before 2016, technical guidance for rope access was set out in Director-General Decree 45/DJPPK/IX/2008. It is still often used as a technical reference for rope access procedures, while Regulation 9/2016 is the broader legal umbrella for all work at height.
